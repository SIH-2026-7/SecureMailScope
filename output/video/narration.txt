Think about what passes through email every day: password resets, business documents, and private conversations. Now imagine an employee logging into their mailbox, but the connection sends their login details before encryption begins. Someone able to observe that traffic could capture those credentials. The email service might still work normally. To the employee, nothing would look wrong.

And exposed logins are only one possibility. A connection might use outdated encryption, present an expired security certificate, or fail to switch to encryption when it should. These weaknesses can put accounts and sensitive communication at risk. That’s why “the email was delivered” isn’t enough. We also need to know whether the connection carrying it was properly protected.

But answering that isn’t straightforward. The evidence can be scattered across thousands of network packets. Someone has to reconstruct the conversation and identify where protection began—or where it failed. The challenge is turning that traffic into clear answers: what happened, why does it matter, and what should we fix?

That’s what we’re building SecureMailScope to help with. Think of it as a safety inspection for email connections. You provide a recording of network traffic, and it reconstructs the connections, checks for observable weaknesses, and presents explained findings. You get supporting evidence, suggested fixes, and an exportable report. Analysis runs locally, and reports leave out credentials and message bodies. The goal is to make hidden connection risks visible, understandable, and actionable.

Now, let’s look under the hood. This cleartext authentication sample is a generated laboratory capture, processed through the same pipeline as an uploaded PCAP or PCAPNG file. The backend extracts packets using TShark, with Scapy as a fallback. It reconstructs TCP streams, tracks retransmissions and gaps, and parses SMTP, IMAP, and POP3 conversations. That sequence lets us check whether authentication happened before or after encryption.

In this session, authentication occurs before encryption, triggering the critical AUTH-001 rule. We can trace the finding to its originating frame, inspect the redacted command, and review the recommended fix. The STARTTLS failure scenario demonstrates a related problem: the client requests encryption, the upgrade fails, and authentication continues in plaintext. Requesting protection doesn’t mean it was established. We check the observed conversation.

SecureMailScope also checks observable TLS versions, cipher suites, and forward secrecy. When a certificate is visible, it checks expiry against the capture timestamp, hostname matching, key strength, and chain trust. There are visibility limits. TLS 1.3 encrypts certificate contents, so those details may remain unknown. Incomplete sessions remain unassessed rather than receiving a misleading clean score.

Alongside explicit rules, we provide a separate machine-learning signal. A Random Forest classifies risk, while an Isolation Forest flags unusual combinations of normalized cryptographic features. The models analyze these features, not raw email content. They use synthetic training data, so they demonstrate the approach without establishing real-world accuracy. The score displays rule deductions and a separate machine-learning penalty, allowing us to inspect how the result was calculated.

Finally, we can export the assessment as JSON, HTML, or PDF, including evidence references and recommended fixes. SecureMailScope connects packet-level observations to conclusions people can inspect and act on—helping us look beyond whether email works, and understand how securely it connects.
